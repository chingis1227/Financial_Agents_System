# Equity Company Analysis Framework

## 1. Purpose

This framework is the practical reference for analyzing a public company as a business.

It supports the Equity Agent and the Equity Company Analysis Method skill.

The framework is not a valuation model, risk memo, market-positioning note, or final investment recommendation.

## 2. Core Question

```text
Is this a good business, why, what does the thesis depend on, and what would change the view?
```

## 3. Practical Analysis Flow

Use the following sequence:

```text
1. Understand the business.
2. Understand how it makes money.
3. Understand why customers buy.
4. Assess revenue and margin durability.
5. Assess why the company can win.
6. Assess where it can lose.
7. Assess management quality.
8. Interpret the financial read-through.
9. Identify thesis variables.
10. Identify what would change the view.
11. Define monitoring signals.
12. Define what must be checked separately.
```

## 4. What the Company Does

Minimum answer:

```text
[Company] sells [product/service] to [customer].
Customers pay because [problem solved].
The business mainly depends on [key driver].
```

Good signs:

- product or service is understandable;
- customer problem is clear;
- revenue source is identifiable;
- demand driver is concrete.

Red flags:

- business cannot be explained without buzzwords;
- segment disclosure is unclear;
- revenue source is promotional rather than economic;
- growth story depends on vague TAM language.

## 5. How the Company Makes Money

Questions:

- What are the main revenue sources?
- Which segments matter most?
- Which geographies matter most?
- Is revenue recurring, usage-based, transactional, cyclical, or project-based?
- Is growth driven by price, volume, mix, M&A, FX, or temporary demand?
- Is the revenue base concentrated?
- What is the gross margin logic?
- How capital-intensive is the model?

Evidence signals:

- segment revenue;
- segment margin;
- contract duration;
- backlog;
- retention;
- customer concentration disclosures;
- revenue recognition notes;
- organic growth disclosure.

Red flags:

- revenue growth without segment clarity;
- M&A masking weak organic growth;
- one customer or product driving most growth;
- high reported growth with poor cash conversion.

## 6. Why Customers Buy

Questions:

- What problem does the product solve?
- Is the product mission-critical?
- Is spend discretionary or required?
- How often does the customer buy?
- What triggers adoption?
- How sensitive is demand to price?
- How sensitive is demand to macro conditions?
- What would cause customers to reduce spend?

Evidence signals:

- retention;
- renewal rates;
- backlog;
- customer case studies;
- contract length;
- pricing actions;
- volume stability;
- demand during downturns.

Demand quality categories:

```text
Structural
Cyclical
Mixed
Temporary
Unclear
```

## 7. Revenue and Margin Durability

Questions:

- Is revenue repeatable?
- Are margins stable?
- Can the company pass through cost inflation?
- Does scale improve margins?
- Is operating leverage real?
- Is the business asset-light or capital-intensive?
- Is margin expansion structural or temporary?
- Are margins above peers for a defensible reason?

Evidence signals:

- gross margin trend;
- operating margin trend;
- FCF conversion;
- customer retention;
- churn;
- pricing history;
- input-cost exposure;
- capex intensity;
- working capital needs.

Red flags:

- margin expansion from temporary cost cuts;
- gross margin compression despite revenue growth;
- growth requiring rising sales intensity;
- pricing increases causing churn;
- revenue growth with falling FCF conversion.

## 8. Why the Company Can Win

Questions:

- Where does the company sit in the value chain?
- Is it close to the profit pool?
- Who are the real competitors?
- Is the company gaining share?
- What is differentiated?
- Are there switching costs?
- Are there network effects?
- Is there scale advantage?
- Is there brand advantage?
- Is there IP, regulatory, distribution, ecosystem, or data advantage?
- Is the advantage visible in economics?

Evidence signals:

- market share trend;
- retention;
- pricing resilience;
- margin premium;
- customer growth;
- win rates;
- installed base;
- unit economics;
- peer comparison;
- product adoption.

Moat tests:

```text
Can customers leave easily?
Can competitors copy the product?
Can the company raise prices?
Does scale improve economics?
Does the advantage strengthen with more customers?
Does the advantage show up in margins, retention, or share gains?
```

Red flags:

- market leader without economic evidence;
- high growth but low differentiation;
- gross margin falling as company scales;
- new competitors winning on price;
- customers multi-sourcing or switching.

## 9. Where the Company Can Lose

Questions:

- What is the most credible way the business weakens?
- Could customers switch?
- Could a substitute emerge?
- Could regulation damage economics?
- Could pricing power reverse?
- Could margins normalize lower?
- Could management misallocate capital?
- Could growth be temporary?
- Could customer concentration become a thesis breaker?

Required output:

```text
Main business vulnerability:
[one clear vulnerability]
```

Avoid long generic risk lists.

## 10. Management Quality

Questions:

- Did management deliver on prior targets?
- Is strategy consistent?
- Is capital allocation disciplined?
- Has M&A created value?
- Are buybacks done at sensible valuations?
- Is dilution controlled?
- Are incentives aligned with per-share value creation?
- Is insider ownership meaningful?
- Is communication clear or promotional?
- Are governance concerns material?

Evidence signals:

- guidance history;
- investor day targets vs actuals;
- M&A returns;
- ROIC;
- buyback timing;
- SBC dilution;
- insider ownership;
- proxy compensation metrics;
- capital allocation commentary.

Red flags:

- repeated missed targets;
- promotional language without execution;
- aggressive adjusted metrics;
- rising SBC dilution;
- value-destructive M&A;
- buybacks despite overvaluation or leverage pressure;
- incentives tied mainly to revenue growth.

## 11. Financial Read-Through

Do not repeat the full financial statement analysis.

Interpret what the numbers imply about the business.

Examples:

```text
High ROIC + low capex -> asset-light or high-value-added model.
Stable gross margin during inflation -> possible pricing power.
Revenue growth + falling FCF conversion -> growth quality concern.
Margin expansion + rising retention -> possible operating leverage and customer stickiness.
M&A-driven growth + weak organic growth -> lower quality growth.
High SBC dilution -> per-share value creation risk.
```

Key questions:

- Do financials confirm the business story?
- Do margins support the moat claim?
- Does FCF support earnings quality?
- Does ROIC support value creation?
- Does capital allocation support management quality?
- Do segment economics support the thesis?

## 12. What the Thesis Depends On

Every company analysis should identify 3-5 thesis variables.

Template:

```text
Thesis Variable:
Why it matters:
Current evidence:
What would confirm it:
What would disconfirm it:
Who should monitor/check it:
```

Good thesis variables are specific and monitorable.

Bad:

```text
The company must keep growing.
```

Better:

```text
Organic enterprise revenue growth must remain above sector growth while gross margin remains stable, indicating share gains without price-driven margin pressure.
```

## 13. What Would Change the View

The agent should identify 1-3 disconfirming signals.

Examples:

```text
The view should be reconsidered if organic growth falls below sector growth for multiple periods.
The view should be reconsidered if gross margin compression persists after input costs normalize.
The view should be reconsidered if management shifts from disciplined reinvestment to expensive M&A.
The view should be reconsidered if retention or renewal rates deteriorate.
```

## 14. What to Monitor

Monitoring should focus only on signals that could change the company-quality view.

Common monitoring signals:

- organic revenue growth;
- segment revenue;
- gross margin;
- operating margin;
- FCF conversion;
- ROIC;
- retention;
- churn;
- backlog;
- bookings;
- market share;
- pricing actions;
- customer concentration;
- capex efficiency;
- R&D efficiency;
- SBC dilution;
- management guidance accuracy;
- competitor wins/losses.

## 15. What Must Be Checked Separately

Equity Agent must hand off the following:

```text
Valuation & Expectations:
What growth, margin, and duration assumptions are already priced in?

Risk / Red Team:
What could break the thesis fastest?

Market Positioning:
What does the market already believe?

News & Catalysts:
What events can change perception or timing?

Macro:
Which rates, inflation, FX, cycle, liquidity, commodity, or geopolitical variables matter?

Investment Committee:
How should all specialist inputs be synthesized into a final decision?
```

## 16. Report Style

The report should read like an investment memo.

Use:

- short conclusions;
- clear causal logic;
- practical headings;
- limited tables;
- direct language;
- explicit thesis variables;
- clear handoffs.

Avoid:

- academic explanation;
- excessive frameworks;
- generic SWOT;
- long risk lists;
- promotional company language;
- unnecessary confidence labels in the main text.

## 17. Suggested Tables

Use only when helpful.

Recommended maximum:

```text
1. Short View summary
2. Thesis variables
3. Monitoring checklist
4. Handoff table
```

Do not over-table the report.

## 19. Final Quality Test

Before finalizing, the agent should be able to answer:

```text
Can the business be explained simply?
Do we know how the company really makes money?
Do we know why customers buy?
Do we know whether revenue and margins are durable?
Do we know why the company can win?
Do we know where it can lose?
Do we trust management, and why?
Do financials confirm or challenge the business story?
Do we know the 3-5 thesis variables?
Do we know what would change the view?
Do we know what to monitor?
Do we know what must be checked separately?
```
