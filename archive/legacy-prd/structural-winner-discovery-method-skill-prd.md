# Structural Winner Discovery Method — Skill PRD

## 1. Purpose

The Structural Winner Discovery Method is a reusable analytical skill for identifying public companies that may become long-term structural winners within a theme, industry, value chain, or similar-company pattern.

The skill converts broad investment themes into a disciplined screening method. It helps agents identify bottleneck controllers, picks-and-shovels suppliers, platform compounders, mission-critical niche leaders, and hidden second-order beneficiaries while avoiding hype-driven false positives.

The skill should be usable by:

- Structural Winners Discovery Agent;
- Theme / Opportunity Discovery workflows;
- Sector / Industry Agent;
- Equity / Company Deep Dive Agent;
- Investment Committee Agent as a reference for interpreting candidate quality.

## 2. Core Philosophy

Major long-term equity winners often emerge where structural change creates durable value capture, not merely where a market sounds exciting.

Core logic:

```text
New technology or structural change
→ becomes required infrastructure
→ creates bottlenecks or control points
→ is controlled by a limited number of companies
→ converts into revenue, margin, cash flow, or platform power
→ compounds over time if value capture remains durable
```

The method must explicitly avoid hindsight bias. Past winners are pattern references, not proof that a current candidate will repeat the same outcome.

## 3. Method Boundaries

The skill is a discovery and screening method.

It does not:

- produce final buy/sell recommendations;
- guarantee future winners;
- label any company as the next version of a past winner;
- perform full valuation models;
- perform full financial statement analysis;
- replace downstream deep-dive work.

It does:

- identify structural winner candidates;
- map value chains;
- identify bottlenecks and control points;
- separate business quality from stock attractiveness;
- apply evidence discipline;
- identify false positives and traps;
- produce candidate cards and next-analysis recommendations.

## 4. Default Analytical Sequence

```text
1. Define the theme or industry.
2. Explain why the theme matters now.
3. Map the value chain.
4. Identify where value can accrue.
5. Identify bottlenecks, scarce capacity, and control points.
6. Identify obvious beneficiaries and hidden second-order beneficiaries.
7. Build a candidate universe.
8. Apply hard disqualifiers and negative screens.
9. Classify candidates by archetype.
10. Assess evidence and thesis maturity.
11. Evaluate demand quality and customer evidence.
12. Evaluate moat / bottleneck strength.
13. Run financial quality sanity check.
14. Run valuation sanity check.
15. Run red-team and disruption test.
16. Rank candidates by tier.
17. Write candidate cards.
18. Recommend next analysis.
```

## 5. Theme Interpretation

The method should translate the user’s theme into a concrete economic question.

Examples:

```text
AI power demand → Who supplies the physical and electrical infrastructure required for data-center growth?
Defense spending → Which companies own critical production capacity, components, electronics, propulsion, or sensing capabilities?
Robotics → Which suppliers control sensors, actuators, machine vision, precision components, or software platforms?
Nuclear renaissance → Which companies control uranium, enrichment, engineering, components, sensors, valves, or specialized services?
```

The method should not stop at the headline theme. It should ask where money actually flows and who can capture margins.

## 6. Value-Chain Mapping

Map the theme into value-chain layers:

- end-market demand;
- direct product or service providers;
- enabling infrastructure;
- critical components;
- scarce capacity;
- software / platform / standards;
- distribution or channel control;
- customer capex and procurement flows;
- regulatory or certification bottlenecks;
- maintenance, service, and recurring revenue layers.

For each layer, ask:

- Is this layer necessary for the theme to scale?
- Is the layer capacity-constrained?
- Is it technically difficult to replicate?
- Does it have pricing power?
- Does value accrue here or pass through to customers / competitors?
- Are public companies investable in this layer?

## 7. Candidate Archetypes

### 7.1 Bottleneck Controller

A company that controls a constrained or mission-critical part of the value chain.

Signals:

- limited substitutes;
- long qualification or certification cycles;
- high customer dependency;
- high switching costs;
- recurring shortages;
- technical leadership;
- pricing power;
- limited competing capacity.

Subtype: scarce capacity owner.

### 7.2 Picks-and-Shovels Supplier

A company that provides required tools, infrastructure, equipment, components, or services to many beneficiaries of the theme.

Signals:

- benefits from broad capex cycle;
- not dependent on one final winner;
- mission-critical input;
- strong backlog or order visibility;
- diversified customer base;
- increasing content per customer.

### 7.3 Platform Compounder

A company whose product becomes a platform or ecosystem.

Signals:

- APIs, SDKs, developer ecosystem, or integration layer;
- recurring revenue or software attach;
- high switching costs;
- ecosystem partners;
- network effects;
- customer workflow lock-in;
- data or model advantage;
- expanding use cases.

Subtype: standard setter / ecosystem owner.

### 7.4 Mission-Critical Niche Leader

A company with narrow but critical specialization.

Signals:

- small niche today but high strategic importance;
- technical complexity;
- reliability or certification requirements;
- deep customer integration;
- high gross margins or stable margins;
- low customer willingness to switch.

### 7.5 Hidden / Second-Order Beneficiary

A company that benefits indirectly from a theme through enabling infrastructure, components, or secondary constraints.

Signals:

- overlooked by headline narratives;
- supplies key inputs to obvious beneficiaries;
- customer capex flows are moving toward its products;
- market may not yet connect the theme to the company;
- evidence is real but less obvious.

## 8. Positive Screening Criteria

Look for companies with some combination of:

- strong position in the value chain;
- bottleneck control or scarce capacity;
- mission-critical product or service;
- high or rising switching costs;
- customer dependency;
- evidence of pricing power;
- increasing customer capex directed toward the company’s category;
- improving margin structure;
- durable R&D or technical advantage;
- long-term management orientation;
- platform or ecosystem evolution;
- recurring revenue, service attach, or software attach;
- operating leverage potential;
- strong balance sheet or credible funding path;
- underappreciated value capture;
- credible reason market expectations may be too low.

## 9. Negative Screening Criteria

Reject or downgrade companies with:

- weak value capture;
- no bottleneck or weak moat;
- easy substitution;
- low switching costs;
- commodity economics;
- poor customer willingness to pay;
- theme exposure only through marketing claims;
- subsidy-dependent demand without durable economics;
- chronic cash burn without credible path;
- repeated dilution;
- weak balance sheet;
- governance or fraud concerns;
- valuation that already assumes extreme success;
- customer concentration that creates fragility;
- capacity expansion that can quickly eliminate scarcity;
- technology substitution risk that is not acknowledged.

## 10. Hard Disqualifiers

Hard-disqualified companies should not be Tier 1 candidates.

Hard disqualifiers:

- fraud or serious governance red flags;
- no investable access;
- unusable liquidity;
- no real revenue unless explicitly early deep-tech / speculative;
- repeated extreme dilution without credible path;
- going-concern risk;
- no evidence of customer willingness to pay;
- theme exposure only through promotional language;
- structurally commodity economics without bottleneck control;
- dependence on one-time subsidies without durable demand.

Override rule:

```text
If included despite a disqualifier, the company must be labeled Speculative or Watch-only, with the reason clearly stated.
```

## 11. Evidence Hierarchy

### Tier 1 — Primary / Official Sources

Preferred:

- company filings;
- annual reports;
- quarterly reports;
- investor presentations;
- earnings call transcripts;
- company segment data;
- official customer filings or capex plans;
- government statistics;
- regulator data;
- ETF issuer data for basket / holdings context;
- exchange or official market data.

### Tier 2 — Reputable Secondary Sources

Acceptable when primary data is unavailable or needs context:

- Reuters;
- Bloomberg;
- Financial Times;
- Wall Street Journal;
- S&P Global;
- Morningstar;
- reputable industry research;
- reputable financial data providers.

### Tier 3 — Narrative / Sentiment Sources

Use only as idea triggers or sentiment signals:

- podcasts;
- YouTube;
- social media;
- Reddit;
- Substack;
- informal newsletters;
- interviews without corroboration.

These must not be treated as verified factual evidence without stronger support.

## 12. Freshness and Missing Data Rule

The method must use the freshest reliable data available when data freshness matters.

Freshness matters for:

- latest financial results;
- valuation and market capitalization;
- backlog, bookings, orders, and capex;
- customer evidence;
- capacity expansion plans;
- supply constraints;
- recent thesis-relevant news;
- analyst coverage, ownership, or attention signals when used.

If direct data is missing, use the freshest reliable alternative or proxy.

Rules:

- state when direct data is unavailable;
- label proxies clearly;
- do not present proxies as original data;
- prefer official and primary sources;
- use reputable secondary sources when primary sources are unavailable;
- lower evidence confidence when data is indirect, stale, or incomplete;
- record material data limitations in the output.

Example wording:

```text
Direct segment-level revenue for the theme was not disclosed. The analysis uses the latest company commentary, customer capex data, and backlog trends as proxies. Evidence confidence is Medium.
```

## 13. Customer Evidence Standard

Prioritize customer-side evidence whenever possible.

Customer evidence includes:

- capex plans;
- procurement commentary;
- multi-year contracts;
- customer dependency statements;
- supply shortage comments;
- design wins;
- backlog conversion;
- customer concentration;
- customer quality;
- evidence that customers are paying or increasing spend.

Distinguish:

```text
Customer says it matters
Customer is spending money
Customer is dependent
Customer cannot easily switch
```

The last three are stronger evidence than the first.

## 14. Business Quality vs Stock Attractiveness

The method must separate business quality from stock attractiveness.

Questions:

```text
Can this company become or remain a structural winner?
Is the current stock setup reasonably attractive, or does valuation already assume too much?
```

A company may be:

- high business quality but unattractive stock setup;
- emerging but not yet financially proven;
- cheap for good reasons;
- expensive but still worth deep-dive if the market misses a new growth leg;
- structurally interesting but not investable yet.

## 15. Valuation Sanity Check Method

The valuation sanity check should be practical and non-model-heavy.

Check:

- market capitalization;
- enterprise value;
- revenue base;
- growth rate;
- margin profile;
- free cash flow or cash burn;
- multiples relative to peers;
- scale of serviceable opportunity;
- implied revenue / margin assumptions;
- recent re-rating;
- whether upside requires heroic assumptions.

Use:

```text
Stock attractiveness: Attractive / Fair / Stretched / Excessive / Unclear
```

The method should not produce a formal price target.

## 16. Demand Quality Method

Strong demand quality signals:

- customer capex rising in the relevant category;
- backlog or orders growing;
- multi-year contracts;
- shortage comments;
- customer dependency;
- high-quality customers adopting the product;
- recurring or expanding spend;
- spending driven by economic need rather than hype.

Weak demand quality signals:

- press releases without revenue;
- broad statements about a large future market;
- pilots without conversion;
- subsidy-only demand;
- customers can delay purchases without consequence;
- customers can switch easily.

## 17. Moat / Bottleneck Strength Method

Strong signals:

- technical differentiation;
- long qualification cycles;
- certification or regulatory barriers;
- scale advantages;
- scarce capacity;
- customer integration;
- switching costs;
- ecosystem lock-in;
- proprietary data, process, or tooling;
- durable cost advantage;
- limited substitute supply.

Weak signals:

- easy replication;
- commoditized product;
- customer dual-sourcing pressure;
- high bargaining power of buyers;
- low switching costs;
- capacity expansion by competitors;
- price competition;
- no clear differentiation.

## 18. Management and Incentive Method

For shortlisted candidates, assess management quality and incentives.

Quick check:

- founder-led or owner-operator signals;
- insider ownership;
- capital allocation record;
- R&D or capex consistency;
- long-term strategic clarity;
- compensation alignment;
- promotional behavior;
- governance red flags.

Tier 1 deeper check:

- compare past management statements to later execution;
- assess whether short-term profit was sacrificed for durable position;
- evaluate whether management understands the structural opportunity;
- look for disciplined capacity expansion or platform investment.

## 19. Historical Analogy Rules

Historical analogies are useful but dangerous.

Allowed use:

- identify patterns;
- compare value-chain position;
- compare platform / bottleneck / picks-and-shovels dynamics;
- compare failure modes.

Required caveats:

- analogy is not evidence;
- past winners are not return forecasts;
- compare failed analogues, not only successful ones;
- explain what is different now;
- explain why the analogy may fail;
- avoid “this is the next X” language.

## 20. False Positives and Traps

Common traps:

- great theme, weak stock;
- large TAM, weak value capture;
- revenue growth without margins;
- capex beneficiary with poor economics;
- hardware growth without software or service attach;
- subsidy-dependent growth;
- customer concentration mistaken for demand quality;
- commodity supplier mistaken for bottleneck controller;
- hype without paying customers;
- promotional small-cap story;
- expensive established winner with no underappreciated upside;
- weak business hiding behind a strong industry narrative.

## 21. Red-Team Method

For shortlisted candidates, ask:

```text
Why might this not become a structural winner?
```

Red-team checks:

- can the bottleneck disappear?
- can customers insource?
- can competitors expand capacity?
- can the product commoditize?
- can pricing power collapse?
- can technology substitute the product?
- can regulation change economics?
- can geopolitics impair access?
- can margin expansion fail?
- can valuation already discount success?
- can the company grow but shareholders not benefit?

## 22. Candidate Ranking Method

Use tiers rather than numeric scores.

```text
Tier 1 — High-Priority Structural Winner Candidate
Tier 2 — Promising but Unresolved Candidate
Tier 3 — Speculative / Early Candidate
Watch-only — Interesting but Not Yet Investable
Rejected — Weak Fit or Failed Screen
```

Tier 1 requirements:

- strong structural fit;
- credible evidence;
- plausible value capture;
- no hard disqualifier;
- financial viability not obviously broken;
- valuation not clearly excessive;
- risks clearly identified.

## 23. Candidate Card Method

Top candidate cards should be concise and readable.

Required fields:

- company, ticker, exchange, country;
- tier;
- stage: Established / Emerging / Speculative;
- archetype;
- one-line verdict;
- why it matters;
- key evidence notes;
- evidence confidence;
- thesis maturity;
- business quality;
- stock attractiveness;
- what the market may be missing;
- what may already be priced in;
- killer question;
- what must be true;
- what would break the thesis;
- what would change the view;
- recommended next analysis.

The “killer question” should be the central unresolved issue.

## 24. Output Clarity Rules

Rules:

- start with a plain-language summary;
- avoid excessive tables;
- use candidate cards for top names;
- use one compact table only for remaining longlist if needed;
- explain jargon when necessary;
- add “So what?” implications after key sections when helpful;
- make the top candidates clear within the first page;
- separate facts, interpretation, and hypotheses.

## 25. Handoff Method

Recommend next analysis, but do not trigger it automatically.

Potential handoffs:

- Financial Statement Analysis;
- Valuation & Expectations;
- Risk / Red Team;
- Company / Equity Deep Dive;
- Investment Committee memo.

Handoff examples:

```text
Run Valuation & Expectations first: the stock has already re-rated and expectations may be demanding.
Run Financial Statement Analysis next: margin durability and cash conversion are the main unresolved questions.
Run Risk / Red Team first: substitution and customer vertical integration are the key uncertainties.
No deep dive yet: customer evidence is insufficient.
```

## 26. Anti-Hype Language Rules

Do not write:

- next NVIDIA;
- guaranteed winner;
- buy now;
- 10x candidate;
- must-own stock;
- once-in-a-lifetime opportunity;
- certain compounder.

Preferred phrasing:

- structural winner candidate;
- high-priority deep-dive candidate;
- promising but unresolved;
- attractive business, valuation stretched;
- watchlist only until evidence improves;
- speculative candidate with meaningful execution risk.

## 27. Active Reference Framework

The method uses the active reference file:

```text
structural-winner-discovery-framework.md
```

The framework is the canonical home for long-form reference logic, including picks-and-shovels investing, bottleneck control, scarce capacity, value capture, platform / ecosystem logic, smart customer demand, R&D and long-term management, 5-15 year horizon discipline, false positives, and traps.

The method skill should apply that reference without duplicating the full framework. Any future changes to structural-winner reference logic should be made in `structural-winner-discovery-framework.md` first, then summarized here only if the execution sequence changes.

## 27A. Additional Method Rules

### Time Horizon Handling

The method should distinguish three horizon buckets:

```text
3–5 years: nearer-term commercial acceleration / earnings inflection
5–10 years: default structural winner horizon
10–15 years: infrastructure, energy, deep-tech, or platform transitions that require long capex/adoption cycles
```

The default analytical horizon is 5–10 years unless the user specifies otherwise. The method should state when a thesis is actually a 10–15 year infrastructure or deep-tech thesis, or when the opportunity depends on a nearer 3–5 year earnings inflection.

### Evidence Output and Optional Evidence Pack

The standard method should keep the main memo readable: compact evidence notes, not a source dump.

For deep runs, the method should support a separate:

```text
evidence_pack.md
```

The evidence pack should record fuller source details, including source, date accessed, publication date or observation period where relevant, key fact/data point, why it matters, and limitations. Missing direct data should be recorded explicitly, and proxies should be labeled as proxies.


### Candidate Universe Size

When the method is used for discovery, the longlist should remain bounded and readable. The default maximum is up to 30 companies, adaptive by theme breadth:

- narrow industry: 8–15 longlist candidates;
- medium theme: 15–25 longlist candidates;
- broad theme: up to 30 candidates.

### Crowding, Attention, and Price Action

The method should include an attention / crowding check for top candidates when data is available. Analyst coverage, institutional ownership, media attention, ETF/thematic flows, and valuation re-rating may be used as supporting signals.

Crowding is a caution, not an automatic rejection. A widely recognized leader can still compound, but extreme expectations may reduce future return asymmetry.

Price action may be used only as a market-recognition signal. The method may note that a stock has materially re-rated or that expectations may have moved ahead of fundamentals, but it must not perform technical analysis, chart-pattern analysis, support/resistance work, or trading-entry recommendations.

### Recommendation Boundary

The method provides deep-dive priority and cautious preliminary stance only. It provides no final buy/sell recommendation and no position sizing.

### Portfolio Fit Exclusion

This method does not perform portfolio-fit analysis, position sizing, allocation advice, correlation treatment, or current-holdings analysis. Those tasks belong to a Portfolio Fit Agent or Investment Committee workflow.

### Agent-Specific Universe Defaults

When this method is used by the Structural Winners Discovery Agent, it should follow the agent-level universe rules: global public companies, US-listed priority, no ADRs as investment candidates, private companies as ecosystem context only, and ETFs only as separate proxy / basket exposure.
